How Timezones Actually Work

Chronosphere Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 4 min read

Until the 1800s, every town kept local solar time: noon was simply the moment the Sun stood highest overhead. That worked for villages — and collapsed the moment trains and telegraphs tried to coordinate schedules across hundreds of towns, each with its own "noon."

The railway fix

Britain adopted a single "Railway Time" (Greenwich Mean Time) in 1847, and within a generation most of the industrialized world followed. Standard time was not a scientific discovery; it was a scheduling agreement.

The 24-wedge model

Earth rotates 360° in roughly 24 hours, so it turns 15° of longitude per hour. The idealized model divides the planet into 24 wedges of 15°, each keeping a whole-hour offset from a common reference.

UTC: the anchor

That reference is UTC — atomic-clock time tied to the prime meridian (0° longitude). Every zone is expressed as UTC plus or minus an offset, from UTC−12:00 to UTC+14:00.

Reality bends the wedges

In practice, zones follow borders, not meridians. China spans about five geographic hours but keeps one clock. India sits at +05:30. Provinces zigzag zone lines to stay with their capital. The pretty 24-wedge map is a teaching tool; the real map is political.

The IANA Time Zone Database

The authoritative rulebook is the tz database (the "IANA database"), which names every region — Asia/Kolkata, America/New_York — and encodes its entire history of offsets and daylight-saving changes. Your phone, your server and Chronosphere all consult it.

Why offsets aren't always whole hours

Some countries chose the mean solar time of their own territory rather than the nearest whole meridian, producing :30 and even :45 offsets. Offsets are data, never assumptions.
