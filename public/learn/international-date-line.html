The International Date Line, Explained

Chronosphere Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 4 min read

The International Date Line (IDL) sits near the 180° meridian. Cross it westward and your calendar jumps a day forward; cross it eastward and you repeat a day.

It zigzags on purpose

The line bends to keep countries and island groups on a single calendar — around eastern Russia, Fiji, and the Aleutians.

Kiribati moves the line (1995)

Kiribati realigned its eastern islands to the west side of the line, creating UTC+13 and UTC+14. Kiritimati (Line Islands) is now the first inhabited place to greet each new day.

Samoa skips a day (2011)

To trade with Australia and New Zealand, Samoa and Tokelau jumped from the east side to the west side — deleting Friday, 30 December 2011 from their calendars entirely.

Three dates at once?

Offsets run from UTC−12 to UTC+14. Because uninhabited UTC−12 exists, at certain moments Earth technically spans three calendar dates — though only two are inhabited.
