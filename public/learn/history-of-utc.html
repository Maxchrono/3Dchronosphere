From Railways to Atomic Clocks: The History of UTC

Chronosphere Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 4 min read

Greenwich wins (1884)

After Britain's railways popularized Greenwich time, the 1884 International Meridian Conference adopted Greenwich as the world's prime meridian, proposing a 24-hour day of zones. Not every country complied immediately — but the reference point was set.

Atomic precision (1955–1972)

Cesium atomic clocks (1955) redefined the second in 1967. Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) emerged as the civil reference: atomic stability, kept within a second of Earth's actual rotation by occasional leap seconds from 1972 onward.

The database behind everything (1986)

The tz database (the "Olson database," 1986) began encoding every region's offset and daylight-saving history as data. It now powers Linux, Android, iOS and virtually every server clock — including Chronosphere's.

Today

UTC is maintained by international metrology (BIPM). The leap second itself is being retired in favor of a smoother future adjustment — a reminder that even "time" is a negotiated standard.

Every clock on the globe is computed from these standards. Related: how timezones work · daylight saving
