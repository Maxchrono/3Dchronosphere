Why India Runs on a Half-Hour Offset

Chronosphere Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 4 min read

Indian Standard Time is UTC+05:30 — no daylight saving, one zone for the whole country. The half-hour is not an accident; it is a 120-year-old compromise.

Many times before one

British India ran on several local times at once — Madras time, Bombay time, Calcutta time — plus a separate "railway time." Schedules across the empire were an arithmetic chore.

The 1906 unification

In 1906 the administration unified the subcontinent on a single standard anchored near 82.5°E, a meridian close to the geographic middle of British India as it then was. Earth turns 4 minutes per degree, and 82.5° × 4 minutes = 330 minutes — exactly 5½ hours ahead of Greenwich. The half-hour fell out of the geography.

Why it survived independence

After 1947, Pakistan departed to its own +5:00 clock, but India kept IST. The offset also lands neatly between Europe and East Asia — convenient for trade, railways, broadcasting and, later, software.

The ongoing debate

India stretches across nearly two geographic hours. In the far northeast the Sun rises early relative to the clock; studies and parliamentary committees have periodically proposed a second zone (+06:00) for the east. So far, "one country, one time" has won — a single zone keeps timetables and databases simple.

Try it: open the globe and search Kolkata or Bengaluru — both resolve to Asia/Kolkata, UTC+05:30. Related: strange offsets · China's single zone
