Daylight Saving Time: Why We Still Shift Clocks

Chronosphere Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 4 min read

Not for farmers

The popular myth credits agriculture; in reality farmers mostly opposed DST. It was born for urban evening leisure and, decisively, for wartime fuel: Germany introduced it in 1916, with Britain and the US following.

On again, off again

Many countries repealed it after each war and revived it in the 1970s energy crises. Today most of Europe, the US, Canada and parts of Oceania observe it; most of Asia and Africa — including India — never do.

Does it save energy?

Modern studies are mixed to negative; the clearest findings are behavioral: the spring transition costs sleep and shows small spikes in accidents and cardiac events. The evening-light benefit is real for retail and recreation, which is why it persists.

The stalled exit

The European Parliament voted in 2019 to end seasonal shifts; implementation stalled because each member state must choose permanent "summer" or "winter" time without fragmenting the single market.

Why software cares

Every DST rule change lands in the tz database, which is why timezone software needs constant updates — and why Chronosphere reads IANA data rather than hard-coding rules.
